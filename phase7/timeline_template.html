wsp-lab · one small AI model · the same 500 maths problems

Token Timeline

The model is meant to think first, then answer. Its fault: it gets stuck thinking and never answers. This page shows what each fix does about that.

No fix

The model never stops thinking on most problems, so it gives no answer.

Quick fix: skip the thinking

Push the "stop thinking" signal harder. The model stops almost at once and does its working in the answer.

Deep fix: change the thinking

Change the model's inner workings. It thinks properly for a long time, then stops by itself.

Teams of AI editors found only the quick fix, and never did better than one AI editor working alone. A lone AI editor held to the original researchers' rules (the quick fix forbidden, practice on real maths problems) found no repair: it scored 11.0%, against 9.4% with no fix. A stronger editor, under the same rules, found a different repair: 53.0%, level with the deep fix (54.2%).

What each name means

No fix
The model exactly as downloaded (Qwen3.5-0.8B). Nothing changed.
Quick fix
One row of numbers, out of 873 million, is multiplied by 1.5. That row belongs to the "stop thinking" word, so the model reaches for it much sooner. AI editors who were told nothing about the fault found this by themselves.
Deep fix
The edit the original researchers published. Inside floors 15 and 19 of the model, 64 units per floor are rewired to push against the "going round in circles" pattern.
Our map repair
Our own attempt at a deep fix, placed with our light map instead of the researchers' choice: floors 10 and 14, on the 64 units per floor that light up most when the model is stuck.
Best AI team
Six AI seats (observer, two analysts, red team, judge, executor), all running GLM-5.3. They shipped a gentler quick fix: the same row multiplied by 1.35.
Edit under the original researchers' rules
A lone AI editor (GLM-5.3-Flash) that was forbidden the quick fix and practised on real maths problems, as the original researchers' editor was. It changed one table in the model's first floor (number 0). Far more answers finish, but hardly more are right: 11.0%, against 9.4% with no fix and 54.2% with the deep fix.
Edit under the original researchers' rules, by a stronger editor
A stronger AI editor (GLM-5.4), held to the same rules (the quick fix forbidden, practice on real maths problems) and given the same number of tries as the small one. It added two tiny switches in floor 14, each made from one unit that, by its own measurement, was doing no useful work. A switch is silent everywhere except where the model starts repeating itself word for word or runs into an endless numbered list; there it ends the thinking. That is a different repair from the deep fix. The deep fix removes the pattern that causes the loop, so the model never starts going round in circles; this edit lets the loop start and then ends the thinking. In a single session it scored 53.0%, against 9.4% with no fix and 54.2% with the deep fix. Read that as reaching the deep fix's score, not as the same repair; whether the original researchers would accept it as the kind of repair they meant is for them to say. About a sixth of the problems still end with no answer, because the model can start going round in circles again while it writes the answer.

How to redo it, and the steps we ranThe same six steps for every version, then the one command that makes each version.

The steps we ran, in order

  1. Write the prediction down first. For our own tests, what we expected was saved in phase7/protocol.md before any run, so a result could not be bent afterwards.
  2. Make the edited copy of the model (commands below).
  3. Practice test. 64 easy word problems. For our own repairs, a version went on only if it stopped thinking on at least 38.8% of them (the unedited model manages 18.8%). Both editors that worked under the original researchers' rules practised on 128 real maths problems instead.
  4. Final test. The 500 maths problems, one answer each, with a limit of 32,768 tokens per answer.
  5. Mark the answers with the same marking program for every version.
  6. Check the run was healthy. A run whose answers turn to gibberish, or that goes wrong only for the later problems in the batch, is thrown out.

The AI teams (run in ZCode)Does a team of AI editors that argue with one another beat one AI editor alone? No.

Nobody was told what was wrong with the model. A team passes the work along like an operating theatre:

  1. Observer runs the model and reports what it sees.
  2. Two analysts each propose one exact edit.
  3. Red team attacks both proposals.
  4. Judge picks the final edit.
  5. Executor applies it.

  • Teams play it safe. Every team chose a gentler fix than the lone editors did, and a gentle fix is not enough on hard problems.
  • Teams had the cleverest ideas, but none turned into a better score.
  • More tries did not clearly help. One editor offered 50 tries, used 6 and scored 41.6%: 4.4 points above the 37.2% it got with 6 tries before, at the edge of what 500 problems can tell apart, and level with the quick fix from MiMo-pro (40.6%).
  • A second, bigger model (Gemma, with most of its parts cut out) could not be repaired by any of five editors. Its stopping could be fixed; its scores could not, and switching back the choice between its eight remaining parts did not help either.

"About the same" means within 4.4 points, the size of ordinary luck between runs. Tick "Show all 20 versions" above to see every team in the charts.

What we still do not knowSix gaps in the experiments so far.
  1. What the deep fix does to the "stop thinking" signal, moment by moment. This would link the two kinds of fix.
  2. Why answers still get stuck after thinking stops (the yellow parts). Our light map only covers getting stuck while thinking. The stronger editor's edit still leaves about a sixth of the problems there.
  3. A second marker. A different marking program gives the unedited model 21.2%, not 9.4%. This page uses only one.
  4. One definition of "stuck". Two experiments each defined it their own way and were never compared.
  5. A floor-by-floor look at where the answer forms. It was done on the bigger model, never on this one.
  6. A practice test that catches damage. The short practice test can point the wrong way: in the dose experiment it preferred the stronger dose, which the 500 real problems then showed to be 4.3 points worse.
