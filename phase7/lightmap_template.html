Which lights switch on for which job

The model is a tower of 24 floors. Every floor has 3,584 small units, drawn here as dots. A dot lights up when that unit behaves clearly differently during the job you pick. Choose a job, then compare it with another to see whether they share lights.

Strength is how big the difference is, not how sure we are of it (so it is not a confidence interval). Slide right for fewer, stronger lights; slide left to let weaker ones in.

Seating only moves dots around; no number changes. A unit's raw seat on its floor is arbitrary. Grouped seats units with the same favourite job (the job they react to most) together: loop first, then each subject, then hard question, then units with no favourite. Ranked lines every floor up the same way: units that get busiest during the job sit at the front, units that go quietest at the back. The same seat on two floors is still not the same unit.

What a light means

Brightness is an effect size (Cohen's d): how far the unit's activity during the job sits from its activity otherwise, in units of its usual spread. 0.8 is the conventional line for a large difference. It measures size, not certainty, so it is not a confidence interval. Hover a dot for its number and whether the unit gets busier or quieter. "Busier" means its number went up and "quieter" means it went down; a unit's number can be negative, so a "quieter" unit may really be pushing harder the other way.

Where the numbers come from

"Stuck in a loop" compares the model while it repeats itself with the same answers before the repeating began (80 saved answers). The seven subjects and "Hard question" compare the model while it reads 500 maths questions; the three template tokens every question starts with are left out of the averages, because one unit fires hugely on the very first token and made its average track question length. Question length itself is then taken out of every unit before the comparison, so a light here means "differs for this subject", not "differs for long questions".

Seating

The order of units inside a floor has no meaning, so the page re-seats them. Grouped: by the job a unit responds to most (loop first, then each subject, then difficulty, then no strong favourite; favourites are fixed at strength 0.8, so seats do not move when you drag the slider). Ranked: by how much busier or quieter the unit gets during the chosen job, so the same seat on every floor means the same rank. Either way a seat is never the same unit on two floors: unit 17 on one floor has nothing to do with unit 17 on the next.

Wires

A unit on a lower floor writes a signal into the shared stream; a unit on a higher floor reads it. The wire's number is how much of the upper light's strength (same scale as the slider) that one cable accounts for in the chosen job, worked out from the model's weights for every pair of floors. The page keeps the 600 strongest per job.

Labels

Hover a light to see its labels. A label is a measurement, not a job description. A subject label is the same thing as a light: the unit is on for that subject at strength 0.8 or more, with question length taken out. "Hard question" has no label because none of its lights reach 0.8.

The lever layer

The last button is not a job. The deep fix (the original edit) works by pushing the model's inner stream along one direction whenever the model is stuck. The lever layer scores every unit by how hard its own output pushes along that same direction when the model is stuck, compared with before: the change in the unit's activity, times how far its output points that way, as a share of the stream's normal spread along that direction. It counts only what a unit writes straight into the stream, not what travels through attention. It shows which units push, not that switching them off would unstick the model. The direction is borrowed from the original edit, and no prediction was written down before this was measured.

What this cannot tell you

Lights show what goes together, not what causes what. A wire is only the straight cable between two units; signals that travel through attention (the part of the model that moves information between words) are not drawn, and they carry most of it. In a first test, erasing the "stuck" pattern did not unstick the model, while a random push triggered by the loop lights did. Every question here is maths, so "job" is a narrow idea.
